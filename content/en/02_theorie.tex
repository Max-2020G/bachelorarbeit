\section{Nonlinear Optics}
\label{sec:NLO}
In optics, most phenomena are linear in nature, meaning that when an external electric field
$E$ is applied to a medium, the electric polarization gives a linear response to this applied
field.
However, once the electric field reaches a strength of $~3\cdot10^{11}\,\unit{V/m}$ or more,
nonlinear contributions become important.
Such a field strength can only be reached using lasers, which are used here for this
thesis \cite{Butcher_Cotter_1990}.
The nonlinearity becomes apparent when a medium is approximated semiclassically and the linear
solution is shown first.
The medium is approximated as a positive nucleus with an electron attached to it via a spring.
Here, the positive nucleus can be assumed to be spatially fixed, since the electrons are
considerably lighter.
An electric field is applied to this system.
This system can be approximated, to first order, as a driven harmonic oscillator.
Due to the Coulomb interaction between the electron and the $E$ field, this yields the
equation of motion
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:bew}
    m\left(\frac{\text d^2x}{\text d t^2}+2\Gamma \frac{\text d x}{\text d t}+\Omega^2\cdot x-\left(\xi^{(2)}x^2+\xi^{(3)}x^3+\dotsb\right)\right)= -e E(t)\,.
\end{equation}
Here, $m$ is the mass of the electron, $x$ the displacement from the equilibrium position,
$\Gamma$ the damping constant,
$\Omega$ the natural frequency of the system, and $\xi$ the anharmonic force terms.
In the linear case, only the harmonic solutions, i.e. $xi^{(i)}= \forall i \ge 2$, are used.
%More details on this can be found in \cite{Butcher_Cotter_1990}.
This is the difference to the nonlinear approach, since there the anharmonic terms cannot
be neglected.
Figuratively speaking, in this case the spring connecting the positive nucleus and the electron
is stretched so far apart that the spring's response is no longer linear.\\
The polarization $P$, in turn, cannot be represented so easily in the spring picture.
The polarization describes the response of the medium to an
incoming electric field.
Mathematically, this is described as $P(t)= -N\cdot e\cdot x(t)$, with $N$ as the particle
density, $e$ as the charge of the electron, and $x(t)$ as the displacement of the electron
from the equilibrium position.
Using the solution of \eqref{eqn:bew} in the linear, harmonic case, the polarization
\begin{equation}
    P =  \epsilon_0 \chi^{(1)}E
\end{equation}
can thus be determined.
In the non-harmonic, i.e. nonlinear, case, however, this cannot be determined exactly, and a
Taylor series expansion is applied.
As a result
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:NLP}
    P = \epsilon_0 \left(\chi^{(1)}_{i,j_1}E+\chi^{(2)}_{i,j_2}E^2+\chi^{(3)}_{i,j_3}E^3+\dotsb\right)
\end{equation}
follows. Here, $\chi^{(1)}$ describes the linear susceptibility, and the subsequent $\chi^{(2)}$,
$\chi^{(3)}$, describe the nonlinear susceptibilities of the medium \cite{Butcher_Cotter_1990}.
Here, $\chi^{(2)}$ is a third-rank tensor and additionally has the dependencies $i$, which
describes the polarization, and $j$, which are components of the field.
The independence of these components can be reduced through crystal and permutation symmetries.
For the process of optical rectification, the $\chi^{(2)}$ term is decisive.
It is decisive here that this term vanishes in certain cases,
for example for a medium that is inversion-symmetric. \\
This can be shown using the inversion operation as a symmetry transformation \cite[Ch. 5.3.2]{Butcher_Cotter_1990}.
This symmetry property is also referred to as centrosymmetry,
which is present in only 21 of the 32 crystal classes, which are therefore viable candidates
for the generation of THz radiation.

Furthermore, it is advantageous to consider the polarization in the frequency domain, for
example in order to make use of the difference frequency.
For this, it is assumed that the electric field $E$ is composed of several frequency components,
i.e. is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:frq}
    E(t)=E_1e^{i\omega_1 t}+E_2e^{i\omega_2 t} + \dots \ .
\end{equation}
If \eqref{eqn:frq} is then substituted into \eqref{eqn:NLP}, squaring produces further
frequencies such as the sum frequency $\omega_1+\omega_2$, the difference frequency
$\omega_1-\omega_2$, and the frequency doublings $2\omega_1,2\omega_2$.
This is called three-wave mixing, since the two input fields and the one output field are
considered \cite{Boyd2023}.
The problem that now arises, however, is that both beams are generated by the same laser and
therefore do not have different frequency components from which to determine their difference.
Here, however, it is crucial that the laser is an ultrashort pulse laser in the fs range, and
therefore, due to the time-bandwidth uncertainty $\Delta t\cdot \Delta \nu \ge \text{const.}$, has
a large frequency bandwidth.
This ultrashort pulse thus has a large bandwidth of frequencies that can mix with one another in
the three-wave mixing process.

\section{Optical Rectification}
\label{sec:OR}
Optical rectification generates the THz radiation via the nonlinear process of difference
frequency generation.
Optical rectification occurs when a limiting case arises.
This is the case when the two electromagnetic waves that hit the material —
here, two parts of the same broadband near-infrared pulse —
have a very similar frequency, so that the difference $\omega = \omega_1 - \omega_2$ is very small
and lies in the THz range.
For the polarization, in which the two electric fields are considered, this means
\begin{equation}
     P(\omega)=\epsilon_0\chi^{(2)}(\omega=\omega_1-\omega_2)E(\omega_1)E^*(\omega_2) \, .
\end{equation}
This polarization describes the motion of the dipoles generated by the field, whereby these
dipoles in turn generate an electric field.

This polarization is generated by part of the NIR wave but is not fully absorbed, which is
illustrated in \autoref{fig:erz}.

\noindent
The electric field is calculated by first transforming the polarization back into the time
domain via an inverse Fourier transform, after which the generated field can be calculated from
Maxwell's equations via the second derivative \cite{Yu2022}.
This results in the electric field
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:ENL}
    E_\text{THz}\approx \frac{\partial^2P(t)}{\partial t^2}\, .
\end{equation}
In order for the two fields that hit the detection crystal not to produce destructive
interference, phase matching must be carried out beforehand, which is explained in the following.
\section{Phase Matching and Coherence Length}
\label{sec:PA}
However, the lasers in the setup do not generate as ideal an electric field as previously
assumed.
This results in a phase shift between the pump beam and the generated THz radiation.
This makes it possible for destructive interference to occur, reducing the intensity of the
radiation.
On the one hand, the two incoming waves must be in phase, and so must the generated THz
radiation.
To ensure this, birefringent crystals are used as the generating medium.
These have different refractive indices along their different axes, thereby producing a
different phase shift.
To adjust this shift, the crystal can be rotated, which adjusts the
phases of the two fields. \\ \\
It is crucial that the crystal material and the wavelength of
the pump beam are chosen such that the group velocity of the pump beam is as close as possible to
the phase velocity of the THz pulse.
THz radiation is not generated at just a single point within the crystal, meaning the
radiation travels a certain distance and can walk off if the group velocity of the pump beam
is not equal to the phase velocity of the generated THz beam.
What matters are the refractive indices at the wavelength of the pump beam, $n_g^\text{NIR}$,
and $n^\text{THz}$ at the wavelength of the THz radiation, between which
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:brech}
    n_\text g^\text{NIR}(\lambda_\text{Pump})= n^\text{THz}(\lambda_\text{THz})
\end{equation}
must hold. In reality, however, this perfect matching is not possible, and over longer
distances the pulses always walk off from each other, causing the generated pulse to also
spread out, blurring the maxima and minima.
How far a pulse remains stable is given by the coherence length.
This coherence length $l_\text{k}$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:koh}
     l_\text{k}= \frac{c}{2\nu\cdot |n^\text{THz}(\nu_\text{THz})-n^\text{NIR}_\text{g}(\lambda_\text{Pump})| }
\end{equation}
\cite{brunner2014thz}.
Here, $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum, and $\nu$ is the frequency of the generated THz
radiation.

\section{Electro-Optic Detection}
\label{sec:TEOS}
Electro-optic detection is a measurement method with which the generated THz radiation
can be measured with time resolution.
The detection starts in the detection crystal, which must be a non-centrosymmetric
crystal. In this crystal, the THz beam and the detection beam are overlapped spatially and
temporally.
The detection beam has a significantly lower intensity than the THz pulse.
The Pockels effect occurs due to the electric field in the crystal.
This is a second-order nonlinear process, and is therefore also described by \autoref{eqn:NLP}.
This causes the crystal to become birefringent;
the strength of the birefringence depends on the intensity of the THz radiation.
The birefringence rotates the polarization of the detection beam.
Thus, the linear polarization of the light from the laser becomes an elliptical
polarization \cite{zheng2023probing}.
Here, the THz pulse is several ps long, while the detection pulse is only $\approx\SI{30}{fs}$;
as a result, the significantly shorter detection pulse can sample the longer THz pulse.
\autoref{fig:EOS} schematically shows the setup of an electro-optic detection, with the beams
moving from left to right.
The arrows indicate the polarization of the pulse at different positions.
as a change after the ZnTe.

The now-altered detection beam then passes through a $\lambda/4$ plate. This delays the
polarization of one axis by $90°$, which means that the polarization of a linearly polarized
beam would become a perfect circle.
In this case, however, the incoming beam is no longer linearly polarized light, but a slightly
elliptically polarized beam.
As a result, the resulting beam is also elliptical and therefore does not have the same amplitude
along the horizontal and vertical polarization axes.
This becomes important in what follows.
This beam then hits a Wollaston prism, which spatially separates the horizontal and vertical
polarization.
These deflected beams are directed onto the photodiodes.
If no THz field hits the detection crystal, the polarization before the
Wollaston prism is a perfect circle, and both of these polarization components are identical.
However, the THz field has caused a rotation of the polarization, and the components are no
longer identical.
Thus, the intensity at the photodiodes is not equal.
The difference in intensity measured by the photodiodes is proportional to the field strength of
the THz radiation.
This proportionality is given by
\begin{equation}
    \symup{\Delta} I  =  \frac{2\cdot \pi}{\lambda_0}I_0 L n_0^3 r_{41}E_\text{THz}\propto E_\text{THz}\ .
\end{equation}
Here, $\symup{\Delta}I$ is the measured intensity difference at the photodiodes, $I_0$ is the
intensity of the detection beam before the detection crystal, $\lambda_0$ is the wavelength of
the detection beam, $L$ is the thickness of the detection crystal,
$n_0$ is the refractive index of the detection crystal at the wavelength $\lambda_0$, $r_{41}$ is
the nonlinear coefficient of the ZnTe for the direction through which the beam hits the ZnTe
crystal, and $E_\text{THz}$ is the electric field strength of the THz beam at the position where
it overlaps with the detection beam.
